# The Man from Antwerp

On Sunday the whole island went to Mass at San Pietro Martire, and the Dorin family went too. Lucia sat with the women on the left side of the church. From there she could see the back of Zuane's neck and the way he turned his head every time the door opened.

The man with the blue ring came in late and stood at the back, near the holy water. He did not kneel. When the Mass was over he went out first, and a moment later Zuane went out after him.

Lucia told her mother she had forgotten her rosary and followed them. She was very careful and very quiet and she kept very close to the wall. They stopped behind a warehouse on the Fondamenta, where the boats unloaded wood for the furnaces, and she stopped too, at the corner, with her heart beating in her ears.

"Three hundred ducats a year," the man said. "A house in Antwerp, near the harbour. Your own furnace, and your name over the door. Not your father's name. Yours."

"And the Ten?" Zuane said.

"The Ten are far away. Antwerp is not Venice. Nobody there will ask were you learned your trade."

"They will send someone."

"Let them send. My masters have friends too."

Lucia pressed her back against the wet bricks. She thought of the law that every child on Murano learned before learning to read: the secrets of the glass belong to the Republic, and the Republic does not let its secrets travel. Men who had left had been brought back in chains. Some had not been brought back at all.

Then the man said something she did not expect.

"You cannot make the cristallo alone. You blow it, yes, better than anyone. But who prepares the batch in your house? Who knows the measures?"

Zuane was silent for a while.

"My sister," he said at last.

"Then bring your sister."

Lucia almost laughed out loud. In nineteen years nobody had ever asked for her, not for anything, not even the guild.

That afternoon she went with her father to Venice to deliver a crate of goblets to a merchant near the Rialto, and they loaded the crate on a big flat boat with other crates from other furnaces, and the boatman rowed slowly across the lagoon past the island of San Michele, and her father talked about the price of wood and the price of ash and how the bigger shops always got the best orders, and Lucia listened and said yes and no and thought only about Antwerp, which she had never seen and could not even imagine.

At the merchant's house she watched the servants unpack the goblets and hold them up against the window, one by one. The winter light went through them and came out on the other side without a stain. Nobody looked at her. She did not mind. She thought: I made the light in these. Not the shape. The light.

On the way back the sun went down behind the city and the domes turned the colour of embers. Her father fell asleep against the crates with his cap over his eyes. Lucia stayed awake and watched the water, and she did not know if what she felt was fear or something else, something that had no name yet because it had never been allowed to happen.

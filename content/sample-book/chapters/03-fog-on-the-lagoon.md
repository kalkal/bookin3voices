# Fog on the Lagoon

They were to leave on the night of the new moon. Zuane told her on the stairs, in a whisper, as if the walls belonged to the Ten. A boat would wait at the end of the Fondamenta after the last bell. The man with the blue ring would take them to a ship at Malamocco, and from there to the open sea.

"You don't have to decide now," Zuane said.

"You already decided for me," she said.

For three days she worked as if nothing had changed. She weighed the ash, she ground the stone, she watched the batch melt in the pot until it was clear. Her father did not notice anything, or pretended not too. Once she caught him looking at her hands while she worked, and he was the one who looked away first.

On the third night the fog came in from the sea. It was so thick that Lucia could not see the furnace from the house, only its glow, like a coal wrapped in wool. She put on her shawl and took nothing else. She had nothing that she wanted to take.

She walked along the canal with one hand on the wet wall, counting the doors, because on a night like this it was easy to step into the water without seeing it. Somewhere a dog barked once and stopped. Somewhere a shutter closed.

Zuane was waiting at the end of the Fondamenta with a bundle under his arm. Behind him, on the water, a lantern swung from side to side, and she heard the knock of an oar against wood.

"Come," he said.

"No."

He stared at her. "Then why are you here?"

"To tell you to go," she said sadly and quietly. "And to tell you that I will not."

"He wants you, Lucia. Not me. You know the batch."

"He wants what I know. That is not the same thing." She took his hands. They were warm, as they always were, from the furnace. "Go. You have the best breath on the island. Go and make your glass where they will write your name over the door. In the morning I will tell father that I tried to stop you and could not."

"And you?"

"Someone has to keep the fire."

From the boat, the man with the blue ring called something in a low voice. Zuane looked at her for a long moment, as if he wanted to remember her face exactly, the way he remembered the shape of a cup before he blew it.

Then he got in the boat and the boat went away in the fog and she went home and in the morning she told her father and he was angry and then he was not angry anymore.

That same day, when the furnace was hot, he came to her without a word and put the blowpipe in her hands. It was heavier than she had imagined, and warm along its whole length. She gathered the glass from the pot, soft and bright as honey, and she breathed into it carefully, the way she had watched Zuane do a thousand times. The bubble grew, clear as water, and it held.

# The Sign of the Siren

The bell of San Donato rang before dawn, it was time to wake the furnace. Lucia Dorin pulled her shawl over her head and crossed the courtyard in the dark. The cold came up from the lagoon, through the stones and through the soles of her shoes.

Her father was already inside. Matteo Dorin had slept by the furnace again, on a sack of ash, the way he did when a big order was waiting. He got up when he heard her and said nothing. He never said much before the first glass of the day.

The furnace was the heart of the house. The furnace kept them warm in winter and the furnace kept them poor in summer, when the heat made the work almost impossible and the best orders went to the bigger shops along the Rio dei Vetrai.

Lucia fed the fire with alder wood from the stack by the door. She was not allowed to blow glass. The guild did not write the names of daughters in its books, and the merchants who came to buy from her father never looked at her hands. But she knew the batch better than anyone in the house. She knew how much white river stone, ground fine as flour, and how much ash from the Levant made the cristallo come out clear as water, and how long to leave it in the pot before the last bubbles rose and broke.

Her brother Zuane came in late, as usual. He was twenty, and the old men on the island said he had the best breath in Murano. He could blow a goblet so thin that it sang when you touched the rim with a fingernail. That morning he did not look at the furnace. He kept looking at the door.

"Who are you waiting for?" Lucia asked.

"Nobody," he said, and picked up the blowpipe.

The morning went on and it was cold and nothing happened. Her father shaped the stems, Zuane blew the cups, and Lucia carried the finished pieces to the cooling oven at the back, one at a time, holding them with both hands like eggs.

Toward noon a boat stopped at their landing. A man in a dark cloak stepped onto the stones and asked for Master Dorin. He spoke Venetian with a northern accent, flat and careful, and he wore a ring with a blue stone. Her father went out to meet him, wiping his hands on his apron. Lucia watched from the window. The man did not ask the price of anything. He looked past her father, into the shop, and his eyes stopped on Zuane.

Before he left, he put a few coins on the bench for the trouble.

When the boat had gone, her father stood in front of the furnace for a long time without working.

"What did he want?" Lucia asked.

"Glass," he said. But he had sold him nothing.

That night Lucia struck a match to light the lamp and found Zuane's bed empty. She found him on the landing, sitting with his feet over the black water, looking toward Venice. The lights of the city trembled on the lagoon like sparks at the end of a pipe.

"Do you know what they do to glassmakers who leave the Republic?" she said.

He did not turn around. "They say the Ten send a man after them."

"They don't only say it."

He laughed, but quietly, so that the sound would not carry across the water. "Go to bed, Lucia. I'm only looking."

She went back inside, but she did not sleep. In his hand, turning and turning in the dark, he had been holding a coin that was not Venetian.
